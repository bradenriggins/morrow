# Instructional design for course work

These are design heuristics, not a claim that one method fits every discipline.
Read the educator's objectives, learners, delivery format, schedule, and local
standards. Preserve an established course unless redesign is requested.

## Start with alignment

Build a compact alignment map: outcome → assessment evidence → practice →
resources → rubric criteria. Use observable actions and a clear performance
standard. Match the assessment to the intended skill: recalling vocabulary
alone does not demonstrate clinical judgment, argument, or practical execution.
Flag an outcome that has no practice or evidence, and an assessment that tests
untaught material. Do not silently change approved outcomes or grading rules.

## Make the learner route clear

A module needs a purpose, estimated workload, sequence, and finish condition.
Use a predictable pattern: orient, learn, practice, apply, reflect. Tell learners
what to do, where to submit, what counts as success, and what happens next.
Break long instructions into task steps. Separate required work from optional
support. Do not fragment one task into many clicks without a learning reason.

## Support practice and feedback

Use low-stakes retrieval and spaced revisits where they fit the subject.
Ask learners to explain, apply, compare, or diagnose, not only recognize.
Add worked examples before independent complex work; reduce scaffolding as
learners gain skill. Use plausible misconceptions for distractors, with one
clearly defensible answer where the question requires one. Explain correct
reasoning in feedback. Avoid trick wording and cultural assumptions unrelated
to the outcome. Do not invent evidence that a technique improved this cohort.

For discussions, state the intellectual task, evidence expected, response
quality, and interaction purpose. “Post once and reply twice” is a schedule,
not a learning objective. For projects, use milestones, examples, and a rubric
that describes observable quality. Check rubric totals, criteria overlap,
points, and alignment before proposing a grading change.

## Inclusive design

Use CAST UDL as a design lens: consider engagement, representation, and action
and expression. Offer relevant choices in examples or demonstration when the
outcome permits them. Provide accessible equivalents for media and clear
planning aids. Alternatives must still assess the same intended outcome.
Do not prescribe universal learning styles or lower standards based on a
learner label. Individual accommodations follow institution-approved processes.

## Assessment review

Check coverage, difficulty, wording, answer key, feedback, points, time limit,
attempt policy, availability, accommodations, and dependencies. Distinguish
Classic Quizzes, New Quizzes, and Moodle question types before suggesting an
edit. Read [assessment hazards](new-quizzes-contract.md) and
[write hazards](write-hazards.md). Existing submissions make changes to points,
rubrics, questions, and grading materially different from editing a draft.

## Deliver a useful design recommendation

Give the observed problem, likely learner effect, proposed change, and a small
example. Label assumptions. Separate essential repairs from optional redesign.
For a program review, use the same evidence rubric across courses, preserve
local differences, and report coverage rather than ranking from partial data.

Reference: [CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/).
The operational advice above is Morrow's design guidance; it is not a copied
standard, an institutional policy, or an automated enforcement feature.
